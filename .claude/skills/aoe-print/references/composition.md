# Posters, covers, and series

## Hierarchy at distance
A street poster, a book cover held in the hand, and a conference schedule have different reading conditions. Set the order of message, identification, and details from that context. A provocative title may be allowed ambiguity; the event time should not inherit it accidentally. Test thumbnail recognition separately from close reading. Do not prescribe one point size independent of size, typeface, and viewing distance.

## Specify and verify the hierarchy
Before rendering, assign each content block a role and relative visual emphasis. Compare apparent letter height, weight, line count, block area, contrast, placement, and surrounding space; font size alone does not determine dominance. Separate a true subtitle from logistical metadata such as date, time, and venue. Mandatory content must be legible, but it does not automatically deserve headline emphasis.

When using a visual reference, estimate relationships between title and supporting text from the image, label estimates as approximate, and adapt them to the new physical size and audience. Give the rendering tool explicit relative-scale and grouping guidance. Do not enlarge a short information block just to fill empty space.

At thumbnail scale, check that the intended dominant elements register first and supporting blocks stay subordinate. At reading scale, check that the smaller copy is still comfortable to read. If the two checks conflict, adjust composition, grouping, weight, or available space rather than simply inflating all text. The brief can legitimately make a time or location the main message; do not impose small metadata on such work.

## Composition decisions
Build the relationship between the type and the image before selecting decorative effects. An image can provide subject, counterpoint, texture, or spatial structure; decide which role it serves. Overlap can unify the page but may compete with information. Assess contrast where the letters actually cross the image, rather than on an empty background sample.

A rigid text grid can contrast with an irregular image (AOE-P01). A quiet masthead can leave the image to establish identity (AOE-P03). Dense, repetitive type can function as image texture (AOE-P02); that is a poor default for essential instructions. These are conditional strategies, not a preferred ranking of styles.

Use negative space intentionally around a focal element or between information groups. Density is not automatically a defect. Ask whether it supports discovery, atmosphere, or close reading, and whether the brief permits the resulting effort.

## Type and language
For type selection, line shaping, hierarchy specification, and critique, read [typography.md](typography.md).

Choose role contrast through some combination of scale, weight, width, case, placement, or material—not all simultaneously by default. Tune tracking and line breaks against the actual words. Do not stretch a typeface unintentionally. Confirm fonts are available and appropriately licensed before depending on them. Preserve the language's writing conventions; do not copy vertical Japanese text as an ornamental pattern into unrelated work.

## Series
Define invariants and variables. For example: stable information anchors and type hierarchy, changing image treatment (AOE-P04). Test the system with the longest headline, the least cooperative image, and an unusually dense content item. A system that only works on the best example is not complete. Avoid repeating the same composition when the series needs different functions.

## Review before showing a poster

Inspect the rendered artifact, not just its source or the rationale. Use these questions to select concrete corrections; do not turn them into a score that implies audience testing.

- **First read:** name the element that actually catches the eye at thumbnail scale. Does its scale, color, contrast, and surrounding space support the intended message? An isolated statement title needs an intentional color decision; muted color must earn its role too.
- **Information roles:** can the reader distinguish title, event identity, invitation, and logistics without the explanation? Check weight and grouping within the supporting text, not only headline versus body. Remove redundant copy unless repetition contributes meaning.
- **Alignment and space:** do title lines share a deliberate edge or center? Check visible corner insets, accidental near-touches, and whether large gaps serve a purpose. Use the equal-edge guidance below for corner-anchored information.
- **Material relationship:** do type and imagery belong on the same surface? When applying grain or ink wear, keep exact copy readable and a clean editable master. A clean treatment can be intentional; do not distress everything automatically.
- **Contribution:** what does each line, dot, box, crop, or repeated unit communicate? Remove habitual ornament. Minimal work still needs character and a specific visual idea.
- **Delivery:** check exact copy, clipping, reading-scale legibility, and which layers remain editable. State unverified fonts, physical proof, or import behavior rather than inferring them from a browser preview.

Report the most consequential observed issue and the targeted correction. AOE's editorial judgment is not evidence of audience performance.

## Preserve the poster as one field

AOE's editorial preference is to avoid unnecessary hard background panels, footer bands, cards, and boxes within an already bounded poster. The After Hours footer was criticized for further encapsulating limited space. First organize information through placement, proximity, alignment, weight, and contrast on the shared field. Do not add a container simply because it is a familiar way to separate metadata.

A contrasting panel can still be justified by the concept or a real legibility requirement. Evaluate what it contributes and what it costs: usable space, continuity, image/type interaction, and visual emphasis. A large dark footer can acquire more emphasis than the information it contains deserves. Resolve difficult backgrounds through placement or crop where appropriate before defaulting to a box.

Establish a concise creative vision before execution: what should the viewer experience, what central relationship communicates it, and how will type, imagery, color, surface, and spatial structure support that relationship? This must describe the particular brief, not a list of fashionable adjectives. Review each major choice against it. Remove habitual separators and decorative structures that contradict the vision even if individually polished.

## Build relationships before arranging blocks

Read [composition-studies.md](composition-studies.md) for three contrasting examples. Choose the relationship from the message and actual content, not from a preferred visual trick:

- **Type makes the image:** repeated meaningful units build a larger recognizable form (C01). Test silhouette first, then unit legibility where required.
- **Type and image create a deliberate contrast:** a stable reading structure balances an expressive field (C02). Shared background, scale, edges, and subject can connect them without overlap.
- **Type occupies an image surface:** the subject provides a legible area for the information (C03). Plan the surface and text together; verify material effects do not destroy the copy.

Describe why the relationship communicates the brief. An image below a title is not automatically a failed composition. Overlap is not a required proof of creativity. Conversely, placing well-made parts on the same background does not by itself create a relationship.

## Compose with masses, gaps, and direction

Before detailing, reduce the intended composition to a few major masses and reading areas at thumbnail size. Identify the dominant shape, counterweight, open areas, and directional forces. The counterweight can be a compact text block rather than a second large object. Balance is not synonymous with centering or four evenly filled corners.

Make gaps do identifiable work: separate messages, clarify a silhouette, create a pause, or protect legibility. Assess both the outer margins and internal negative shapes. C01's open upper-left area accommodates a different reading mode; C02's text columns establish predictable gaps; C03's spaces distinguish tiers within a common surface. Do not apply equal spacing indiscriminately to semantically different groups.

Use variation within a recognizable structure. Repeated units can vary in angle or density to imply direction, but uncontrolled variation produces noise. Cropping should reinforce scale or continuity while preserving identifying features and required copy. Distinguish intentional overlap, clear separation, and accidental near-touches; revise ambiguous tangencies that weaken the subject or reading order.

## Turn the layout into the asset brief

Set the poster proportions, approximate dominant mass, protected reading zones, crop boundaries, and direction of movement before making or generating illustration. Regions are composition constraints, not instructions to draw background boxes. Test with the longest actual text before commissioning a surface intended to hold it.

Reinspect the combined artifact. If imagery closes a needed gap or type competes with a focal point, adjust the relationship or regenerate the asset rather than covering the problem with a panel. A matched paper field can help continuity, but cannot replace composition. Preserve an editable master when applying surface effects to text.

## Equal edge insets for corner-anchored information

For quiet information blocks anchored near a poster corner, AOE's default is matching horizontal and vertical outer margins. In Common Ground, the lower-left block should sit as far from the bottom edge as from the left edge. Measure the visible text extent, not just a text baseline or an oversized line box; allow small optical adjustments when font shapes require them. Keep internal line spacing independent of this outer inset. This applies to corner-anchored arrangements like this example, not a mandate to equalize every gap in expressive compositions.

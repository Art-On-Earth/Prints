# Illustration character and surface

## Minimal does not mean generic

Reduction should preserve the distinguishing qualities of the subject: silhouette, proportion, gesture, edge character, and the way parts relate. A small number of shapes can be expressive; additional detail cannot rescue an unconsidered silhouette.

In the rejected After Hours pilot, four technically recognizable chair drawings behaved like isolated generic symbols. Changing chair types and adding small props did not create the energy or authorship the concept promised. Do not equate “original vector paths” with a convincing illustration treatment.

Before producing a full illustration set, develop one representative subject at final-use scale. Decide what gives it character: compressed or exaggerated proportions, intentional perspective, a particular line rhythm, solid-versus-open areas, or an observed material behavior. Test whether the subject remains distinctive without decorative texture. Keep simplification consistent, but do not repeat identical angular distortions on every object.

Choose an image-making method capable of the proposed treatment. Do not default to basic SVG primitives solely because they are easy to author. An independently placed illustration asset can be raster when necessary while the layout and text remain editable. Explain that boundary. For generated illustration, use the available image-generation workflow and generate assets separately from exact typography.

Inspect the subjects as a composition, not as an asset inventory. Consider interaction, overlap, shared direction, cropping, and purposeful unequal scale. Four equal corner objects can resemble a decorated frame rather than a gathering. Symmetry is valid when it serves the idea; do not impose chaos as a cure.

## Design the asset for its place in the composition

Establish the final layout before generating illustration: poster proportions, image coverage, crop boundaries, text zones, and the intended interaction between type and image. Specify those constraints in the asset brief. Do not default to a square illustration and then organize a portrait poster into header/image/footer rectangles to accommodate it. This failed in the After Hours revision.

Choose the asset geometry from its role. A full-field illustration should use the intended poster proportions and deliberately reserve quiet areas for separately typeset copy. A placed subject should have transparent or otherwise compositable surroundings when the tools support it, rather than bringing an unwanted rectangular background. An intentionally framed image can retain a rectangle when that frame serves the concept. No single aspect ratio is always correct.

Request enough surrounding composition for the planned bleed or crop without losing important silhouettes. Inspect the asset in the actual poster before acceptance. If its shape or background forces a conflicting layout, revise or regenerate the asset to fit the vision; do not mask the mismatch with panels, arbitrary cropping, or cosmetic fades. Preserve editable typography independently of generated imagery.

## Grain as a controlled material treatment

Grain can connect otherwise clinical surfaces, soften a flat fill, or suggest an image-making character. It is an available art-direction tool, not a mandatory AOE signature or evidence of physical printing. Distinguish paper variation, image grain, ink dropout, and halftone: they affect different parts of the work and should not be treated as interchangeable noise.

Choose the role before applying it:
- **Paper variation:** low-contrast variation in the substrate, separate from artwork. Preserve light areas and margins where intended.
- **Image grain:** texture confined to a photograph or illustration, with scale related to that image's treatment.
- **Ink variation:** irregular coverage clipped to selected printed-looking shapes. Fine supporting text may need protection.
- **Halftone:** a deliberate dot structure used to represent tone; assess it separately rather than substituting random grain.

### Application procedure

1. Keep a clean master. Add texture as a named, independently adjustable layer or mask; avoid flattening editable text to apply it.
2. Specify where it acts, its particle size at intended output dimensions, density, contrast, and opacity. Start restrained and increase only until its intended effect is visible. There is no universally correct opacity or grain size.
3. Use a repeatable generated texture or an appropriately licensed asset. Avoid obvious tiling, color noise where only tonal variation is intended, and a uniform dirt layer covering every element. Do not claim a simulated effect proves a printing process.
4. Compare clean and textured versions at thumbnail, intended reading size, and close detail. Check that grain contributes to the chosen character without becoming the subject, muddying the palette, closing counters, or eroding thin strokes.
5. Verify in the destination format. Effects that look correct in a browser may import or export differently. If necessary, rasterize the texture layer alone at an appropriate output resolution, retain the editable artwork beneath it, and disclose the raster component. Test the result rather than promising filter compatibility.
6. For print, evaluate with the actual production conditions and proof. Repeated dot patterns may interact with screening; inspect output rather than claiming press readiness from a screen preview.

When hierarchy, illustration, or type selection fails, correct those first. Grain should enrich a viable design, not conceal an unresolved one.

# Typography and hierarchy

AOE working guidance for designers using AI. For the observed examples behind the contrasts here, read [typography-studies.md](typography-studies.md). They inform judgment; they do not establish universal numerical rules or measured audience outcomes.

## Define the reading job

Sort actual copy into what must attract, identify, explain, and enable action. These are roles, not mandatory separate blocks. A date may be the message; an image may lead; a poster may communicate without a conventional headline. Preserve exact supplied wording before proposing edits.

State the intended reading sequence and viewing conditions before choosing sizes. Distinguish a distant glance, a closer read, and a publication held in the hand. Do not make every element readable from the same distance. Do not make essential details effectively unreadable to achieve a quiet look.

## Choose the typographic relationship

- **Image leads, text rewards a closer look:** use a cohesive cluster and clear internal hierarchy; T01 is an example. Check text contrast against the actual image placement.
- **Type and image share a dense field:** give the title a recognizable silhouette and secondary blocks consistent roles; T02. Density requires organization, not automatic simplification.
- **Type carries the composition:** group phrases, tune line breaks, and use a few meaningful alignment anchors; T03. Similar sizes can work when spacing clearly distinguishes groups.

These are possibilities, not three presets that every project must use. Explain why the selected relationship suits this message.

## Choose type by behavior

Test available fonts with the real headline, longest words, numerals, punctuation, and any language-specific characters. Compare width, apparent height, weight, counters, terminals, and rhythm at the intended size. A narrow face can fit long copy but may alter tone or small-size readability; a heavy face can dominate without being tallest. Do not identify an exact font from a screenshot unless verified.

Assign each family a job. Add a second family when its contrast contributes meaning or usability; do not pair fonts simply because a template expects a serif and sans. T01's change of register is purposeful; T03 demonstrates how much one forceful voice can do.

Keep display eccentricities away from essential details when they impair reading. Do not silently accept a fallback font as the final art direction. Confirm actual font availability before building the final editable layout. If substituting, recheck line breaks and composition rather than assuming equal point sizes produce equal results.

## Specify emphasis, not just font size

Judge scale together with weight, width, number of lines, occupied area, contrast, position, and surrounding space. A wide four-line supporting block can overpower a compact headline even at a smaller point size.

For the chosen design, record a provisional type specification: role, actual text, family/style, size and units, line height, tracking, width, alignment, and intended line breaks. Set numerical values for that artifact only, then revise after rendering. Do not extrapolate physical point sizes from screenshot pixels.

When a subtitle competes, first identify why. Reduce its area or weight, change wrapping, regroup it, or increase separation as appropriate. Do not automatically shrink all supporting copy. The rejected pencil pilot calls for compact logistical details in that brief; T02 permits a stronger supporting layer.

## Shape lines and spaces

Break display copy at meaningful boundaries and inspect the resulting block silhouette. Avoid isolating a weak word or severing a name merely to fill a rectangle. Intentional fragmentation may serve the concept; verify readers can reconstruct the phrase. Never change exact copy without making the change explicit.

Tune display leading against actual letterforms: check ascenders, descenders, punctuation, and collisions. Tightness can create a block, but accidental collisions are not expressive intent. Give continuous reading text enough separation to track lines comfortably.

Inspect spacing between letters and words optically, especially large titles and capitals. Apply broad tracking changes only when they improve the phrase; fix exceptional pairs individually where the editing tool permits. Avoid unintentional horizontal scaling to force a fit; try an appropriate width/style, line break, or layout adjustment first.

Distinguish the gap inside a group from the gap between groups. In T03, spaces carry structural work otherwise delegated to size. Use recognizable alignment relationships while allowing deliberate exceptions. Do not normalize expressive steps into a rigid grid solely for neatness.

## Work with imagery and density

Check every text region against the local background, including texture and tonal changes. Move the text, adjust the crop, or simplify the competing area before adding a default backing box. A backing shape is valid when it contributes to the concept, as in the verbal-visual study.

White space is not a quota. Use it to isolate, separate, or pace; use density to accumulate, energize, or invite exploration. If both title and details compete, reorganize their relationship rather than reducing all elements equally. Keep invented filler out of empty areas.

## Critique the rendered artifact

1. At thumbnail size, name what actually attracts attention. Compare it with the intended sequence rather than declaring every large element correct.
2. At intended reading size, transcribe the mandatory information. Check unclear characters, weak contrast, clipping, awkward breaks, and collisions. Report when physical proof is unavailable.
3. Trace group boundaries and alignment anchors. Identify accidental ambiguity separately from deliberate tension.
4. Edit the longest title and a representative date in the master. Ensure text remains editable and the layout survives realistic content changes where reuse is intended.
5. Report up to three consequential findings as observation → communication effect → targeted adjustment. Preserve what works. Render again after fixes; prompt wording alone is not verification.

Do not label work “AI-looking” without explaining the observed problem. Test specificity of the idea, intentional hierarchy, and relevance to the audience rather than using rough texture, unusual fonts, or asymmetry as proof of quality.

## Avoid a default editorial wrapper

In the Open Draft pilot, the designer liked the correction concept but rejected the stock-feeling small uppercase slash-separated masthead, repeated event information, divider, and unexplained dots. These elements weakened this composition rather than organizing it. Before delivery, account for each label, separator, mark, and repetition: what does it communicate or help the reader do? Remove elements whose only justification is filling a familiar layout slot. This is not a universal ban on slashes, rules, dots, serif type, or repetition.

If the concept invokes a physical action, match the visual behavior to that action. A pen correction should not look like a pair of identical straight vector rules. Consider gesture, pressure, endpoints, speed, and imperfect retracing; avoid distributing random roughness over the whole design. Preserve editable text while keeping expressive marks in independent vector layers. Do not claim digitally drawn marks were physically handwritten.

Identify the intended entry point explicitly. The actual event title must be findable; crossed-out conceptual copy should not unintentionally become its rival. Review the relationship at thumbnail scale and with someone who has not read the explanation.

## Weight expresses information roles

Before styling supporting text, distinguish identification (what is it?), invitation or instruction (why engage, or what to bring?), and logistics (when and where?). Importance depends on the reader's question: an event description may need to resolve an ambiguous title, while a date must be easy to recover during planning. Essential information need not dominate the poster, but it must have a discoverable place.

Create hierarchy within the supporting layer, not just between that layer and the headline. A semibold event description can anchor a regular-weight invitation; a semibold date can anchor a compact regular-weight time/location group. This is the Open Draft solution, not a rule that dates always outrank locations. Let the brief determine the anchor. Use spacing to distinguish groups and proximity to connect dependent lines.

Compare apparent emphasis after rendering. A larger regular invitation can overpower a smaller bold identifier; weight, size, and occupied area work together. Avoid making every essential line bold, inventing a different weight for every line, or using faint gray to force hierarchy. Test actual supported font weights rather than relying on nominal values that may render identically. Ask whether someone can identify the event and find when/where without reading every line sequentially.

## Establish a message hierarchy before assigning weights

Bold labels cannot resolve overlapping messages. In the rejected After Hours pilot, the title, “A neighborhood music festival,” and “Live music, food & dancing” became three disconnected announcements. Before styling, ask whether secondary phrases explain distinct things or repeat the same proposition. Group an identifying line and its supporting activity list into one reading unit when appropriate; change or consolidate wording only within the user's copy-editing authorization.

For that event, the intended structure is title → one event-description group → practical attendance group. Date and place need to be retrievable, not promoted into a competing headline. Do not mechanically copy Open Draft's accepted weight assignments into another poster. Judge the whole message, not whether every role has a different nominal weight.

## Typography belongs to the material and focal system

In Common Ground, the designer accepted the image direction but rejected a broad heavy title and the disconnect between textured paper/illustration and perfectly clean text. Decide whether the action, image, or title should attract first before choosing title weight and scale. Do not maximize both by default. A somewhat smaller illustration can create breathing space while remaining the focal subject; judge actual contrast, silhouette, and placement after resizing.

When the material treatment matters, consider restrained ink variation or edge behavior on display type as part of the same vision. Preserve letter recognition, counters, thin strokes, and small text. Keep a live text master and make the effect independently adjustable. Never use roughness to disguise unsuitable font selection. Inspect at intended reading scale and verify destination support for effects.

The designer associates earth/nature with serif possibilities and prefers tall, less squat serif proportions when using them. Treat this as an editorial preference to evaluate, not a universal nature-to-serif rule or a request to use serif for every plant brief. Consider cultural associations, audience, actual letterforms, and image character. Use genuine family widths/styles or deliberate display transformations; assess the resulting letterforms rather than treating tall proportions alone as sufficient.

## Statement titles need deliberate color and alignment

For a large isolated statement title, consider the open space part of its emphasis. In Common Ground, the designer rejected the muted title color and arbitrary stepped alignment. Prefer a clear shared left or right margin, or a deliberate centered axis, for this kind of title. “Align to an edge” means a consistent compositional margin, not placing letters against the trim. Align related supporting information where that strengthens the structure.

A statement title may need a more expressive color than a palette's default quiet ink. Select it in relation to the concept and image, then reassess focal balance and legibility; stronger color can change what attracts first. Do not prescribe saturated color for every title or erase intentional stepped arrangements such as T03. Depart from a common edge only for an explicit compositional reason, not as an automatic decorative offset.

## Research type through real uses
For unresolved font character, consult [Fonts In Use studies](fonts-in-use.md). Compare actual title words in their composition rather than choosing from a generic serif/sans label. Preserve attribution and distinguish available fonts from inspiration.

## Shape display typography deliberately
AOE permits expressive distortion of display lettering: height, width, slant, tracking, pair spacing, and weight are design variables, not untouchable defaults. First use native width/weight/optical-size axes when appropriate; intentional nonuniform scaling is also valid when the resulting form supports the concept. Do not confuse this with accidental stretching to fit a box. Preserve editable text and an unmodified source.

Judge actual words after manipulation: counter openness, repeated-letter rhythm, stroke balance, serif joins, interline gaps, and optical edge alignment. Increasing height also changes horizontal/vertical stroke relationships; tighter tracking can make repeated m/n forms congeal. Fix pairs or reduce the transformation when recognition breaks. Keep essential small copy undistorted unless the brief supplies a clear reason. Test at thumbnail and reading scale. Do not claim a reference was mechanically stretched merely because it is condensed; it may be a deliberately drawn face.

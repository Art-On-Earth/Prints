## C01 — Repetition builds a silhouette

**Observation:** Repeated rectangular word fragments reading HEAT accumulate into a large flame-shaped mass. Fragments vary in angle, overlap, and color: black outer pieces, yellow and orange interior pieces, and darker ochre areas. A match-like vertical stem enters from the bottom edge. Several unequal flame tips rise upward; the tallest reaches toward the upper right. A compact explanatory paragraph sits in the upper left on open orange. Broad orange areas remain around the irregular silhouette.

**Interpretation:** Word and image reinforce the same idea: repetition is the material of the flame, not a second layer added to an independent illustration. The silhouette establishes the distant read; individual fragments reward closer inspection. The small paragraph supplies another reading mode without needing a giant headline. Its quiet placement balances the upward-right movement of the main mass.

**Transfer:** Begin with a legible large-scale silhouette, then use meaningful repeated units to build it. Vary their direction and density in relation to the subject's motion. Preserve negative gaps that distinguish protrusions and keep the shape readable. Keep essential explanatory text outside the expressive accumulation when it requires continuous reading.

**Tradeoffs:** Repeated letters may become texture rather than readable language; that is acceptable for motif units but not automatically for mandatory information. A rectangle is justified here because it is part of the image-building vocabulary, not an arbitrary container for logistics. Do not turn the no-unnecessary-boxes preference into a ban on rectangular forms. Do not copy the flame, palette, and repeated HEAT construction as a ready-made solution for unrelated content. ## C02 — Order above, inward movement below

Source locator: screenshot 2026-10-05 at 9.29.33 PM. The inner dark portrait artwork was inspected; the thick black rounded surrounding panel is presentation context and is not assumed to be part of the original poster.

**Observation:** A short stacked title occupies the upper left. Several compact text columns share an upper alignment, with tiny credits toward the upper right. Below, nested head-like contours diminish toward an inner point, while shoulders and a tie extend to the lower crop. Light grainy rims distinguish dark layers. The figure occupies most of the lower field; text remains on the same dark background without a separate header panel.

**Interpretation:** The text's regular alignment contrasts with the image's irregular concentric movement. Repeated contours direct attention inward. Grain helps separate the contours and establish depth rather than merely decorating empty areas. The title is more emphatic than body columns while staying quiet relative to the figure.

**Transfer:** Allow text and image distinct regions when shared edges, proportion, background, and thematic relationship make them cohere. Reserve the reading area before producing the image. Use nested or repeated forms when inward movement serves the concept, and tune their spacing so the focal region does not collapse into undifferentiated darkness.

**Tradeoffs:** Small pale type and grain can conflict; assess actual reading scale. A centered visual vortex can overwhelm the text. This example directly contradicts a blanket rule that type placed above an image is disconnected: overlap is optional. Do not force type across a figure merely to claim integration. No actual spray, print, or photographic process can be established from the screenshot alone.

## C03 — The image becomes the typesetting surface

Source locator: screenshot 2026-10-05 at 9.29.47 PM. One graphic showing the back of a pale bob haircut with red lettering inspected. The rounded viewer frame and copy button are excluded from the composition analysis.

**Observation:** The head fills most of the portrait field. The short title sits near the crown in large shaped red letters. Other names are centered in separated tiers down the hair, with different scale/emphasis. Red streaks follow the predominantly vertical hair direction. Pale hair provides the lettering's field; dark shoulders anchor the lower edge. Some background remains visible beside the head.

**Interpretation:** The hair is both subject and writing surface, eliminating the need for a separate text area. Width and grouping respond to the head's usable silhouette. Repeated red establishes one information system; gaps separate name groups. Streaks make the text appear related to the material, though physical dyeing versus digital compositing cannot be inferred.

**Transfer:** Identify an image surface that can carry the actual copy. Compose or obtain that surface with sufficient area, local contrast, and suitable texture. Set a readable text master first, then apply controlled masking, distortion, or texture if these support the material relationship. Keep essential letterforms recognizable and retain the editable text separately from any flattened composite.

**Tradeoffs:** Surface integration can reduce readability, especially with long names or moving tonal backgrounds. Test the longest line and smallest information early. Do not infer formal artist billing or popularity from size alone, and do not recreate the distinctive haircut/red-lineup combination for a new campaign.

## What the three cases establish

Integration can mean construction (C01), coordinated contrast (C02), or shared surface (C03). It does not mean compulsory overlap. Each has one strong organizing relationship, and spacing operates within that relationship. They are examples of possible decisions, not proof that the same effects improve every poster.
